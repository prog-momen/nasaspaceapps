Introduction
Detecting and analysing trends in time series data is a crucial aspect of environmental science, particularly regarding global warming and its associated impacts. One significant consequence of rising global temperatures is increased atmospheric methane concentrations, a potent greenhouse gas. While carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the primary driver of global warming due to its higher concentration and longer atmospheric lifetime, methane (CH₄) plays a critical role that cannot be overlooked.
Methane is over 25 times more effective than CO₂ at trapping atmospheric heat over 100 years. Despite its lower concentration than CO₂, its high global warming potential makes it a key target for climate change mitigation efforts. Additionally, methane has a relatively short atmospheric lifetime of about 12 years, meaning reducing methane emissions can have a quicker impact on climate change mitigation than CO₂.
Understanding trends in methane concentrations is essential for developing effective strategies to cure global warming. These trends can provide insights into the effectiveness of emission control policies, the contribution of different methane sources, and the potential feedback loops in the climate system. Given its significant impact and the need for timely action, it is imperative to detect and analyse methane concentration trends accurately.
In this article, we will explore various statistical tests to detect trends in time series data, using the example of global methane concentration increases due to global warming. We will start with the straightforward linear regression approach and move on to more sophisticated methods, including the Mann-Kendall, seasonal Mann-Kendall and unit root tests. Each technique will be illustrated with Python code examples, providing a practical framework for analysing similar environmental datasets. Since these tests allow us to statistically test the existence of a trend in time series data, I will intentionally reduce the trend slope of toy datasets until we can reject the null hypothesis of no trend in global methane concentration.
Understanding and identifying trends in methane concentrations is vital for developing mitigation strategies and informing policy decisions. By applying these robust statistical methods, researchers can gain deeper insights into the temporal dynamics of methane levels and their broader implications for climate change.
Generating toy datasets
Even if we can find the time series data of the global methane concentration on the NOAA website, it is meaningful to generate the dataset to remind us of its current level and seasonal variation. First, we assume the level and slope to be 1800 ppb as of 2000 and 7 ppb/year, respectively. Its concentrations exhibit seasonal variations, typically with higher values in late summer and lower in late winter. The seasonal cycle can vary depending on the geographic region and specific atmospheric conditions, but a common approach is to model the seasonal variation using a sinusoidal function. To generate a toy dataset, we can assume an approximate seasonal variation of about ±10 ppb with a standard deviation of ±5 ppb superimposed as noise.
I generated several datasets using the code below. Each list element of 'slopes' characterises the ‘dataset [i]'. The 'slopes [i][0]' and 'slopes [i][1]' denote the methane's slope (ppb/year) and the existence of the seasonal variation, respectively.
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from collections import namedtuple
np.random.seed(1)
# Parameters for the toy dataset
start_year = 2000
end_year = 2020
years = np.arange(start_year, end_year + 1)
months = np.arange(1, 13)
intercept = 1800  # Starting methane concentration in ppb
seasonal_amplitude = 10  # Amplitude of seasonal variation in ppb
noise_std_dev = 5  # Standard deviation of the noise
# Generate yearly and monthly time points
time_years = np.repeat(years, 12)
time_months = np.tile(months, len(years))
time = time_years+(time_months-1)/12
# slopes = [[slope, existence of seasonal variation],...]
slopes = [[0.1,1], [0.2,1], [0.3,1], [7,1], [0.1,0]]
methane = namedtuple('methane', ['years', 'concentration', 'slope'])
dataset =[]
noise= np.random.normal(0, noise_std_dev, len(time_years))
seasonal_variation = seasonal_amplitude * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (time_months - 1) / 12)
for slope in slopes:
# Generate methane concentrations with a linear trend, seasonal variation, and random noise
methane_concentration= np.round(intercept + slope[0] * (time_years - start_year) + noise \
+ seasonal_variation * slope[1],1)
comment = f'{slope[0]} ppb/year' if slope[1] else f'{slope[0]} ppb/year without seasonal variation'
dataset.append(methane(time, methane_concentration, comment))

Ordinary Least Square Regression
Applying a linear regression aligns with standard practices for analysing time series data to determine if there is a significant trend. The null hypothesis is that the slope is zero, which implies no significant trend, while the alternative hypothesis is not zero, which implies a significant trend. Then, conduct a t-test on the slope coefficient from your linear regression. The
t
-test will help you determine if the slope differs significantly from zero. If the p-value from the
t
-test is less than our chosen significance level (e.g. 0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that there is a significant trend in our time series data. If the p-value is greater than the significance level, we fail to reject the null hypothesis, indicating that there is no significant trend.
I use OLS in ‘statmodels’ in the following code. Because the methane trend is evident, I reduced the slope from 7 ppb/year to 0.2 and 0.3 ppb/year to generate the 'dataset[1]' and 'dataset[2]'. The latter case of 0.3 ppb/year exhibits a significant trend, but the former case of 0.2 ppb/year shows signically no trend. These two limits act as a window to the significance of the trend. If we want to maintain an insignificant trend in the methane increase, we should reduce the slope to 1/35 of the current slope with the noise assumed.
import statsmodels.api as sm
def ols_model(data, plot=True):
# Fit a linear regression model
X = sm.add_constant(data.years)
model = sm.OLS(data.concentration, X).fit()
if plot:
plt.scatter(data.years,data.concentration, label='Data')
plt.plot(data.years, model.fittedvalues, color='red', label='Fitted Line')
plt.xlabel('Year')
plt.ylabel('Methane Concentration')
plt.legend()
plt.show()
# Run regression model
model.summary()
print(f"Yearly data generation slope:{data.slope}")
print(f"fitted slope: {model.params[1]}")
print(f"t-statistic: {model.tvalues[1]}")
print(f"p-value: {model.pvalues[1]}")
if model.pvalues[1] < 0.05:
print("Reject the null hypothesis: There is a significant trend.\n")
else:
print("Fail to reject the null hypothesis: No significant trend.\n")
ols_model(dataset[1], plot=True)
ols_model(dataset[2], plot=False)

4. Test Statistic (Z)
To determine the significance of the trend, the test statistic Z is calculated:
Press enter or click to view image in full size
The value of Z is then compared to the critical values of the standard normal distribution to determine the trend's significance. Typically, a significance level is chosen. If the absolute value of Z is greater than the critical value (e.g., 1.96 for α=0.05), the null hypothesis of no trend is rejected, indicating a significant trend. We can draw the critical value 1.96 assuming the normality of the Z value, which can be justified by the central limit theorem and a simulation using a random generator of normal distribution. The code below shows the distribution of the Z values to follow the standard normal distribution: The histogram results in an excellent fit to the normal distribution with a location of -0.010 and a scale of 0.991.
from scipy.stats import norm
np.random.seed(1)    # for reproducibility
# Function to perform the Mann-Kendall test and return S
def mann_kendall_test(data):
n = len(data)
S = 0
for i in range(n - 1):
for j in range(i + 1, n):
S += np.sign(data[j] - data[i])
return S
# Simulate multiple samples and calculate S
sample_size = 100  # Large sample size
num_simulations = 10000
S_values = []
for _ in range(num_simulations):
data = np.random.normal(size=sample_size)
S = mann_kendall_test(data)
S_values.append(S)
# Standardize S to calculate Z
mean_S = 0
var_S = (sample_size * (sample_size - 1) * (2 * sample_size + 5)) / 18
Z_values = [(S - mean_S) / np.sqrt(var_S) for S in S_values]
# Plot the standard normal distribution for comparison
xmin, xmax = plt.xlim()
x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, 100)
p = norm.pdf(x, 0, 1)
plt.plot(x, p, 'k', linewidth=2)
title = "Histogram of Z values with Standard Normal Distribution"
plt.title(title)
# Fit the Z values to Normal distribution
loc, scale = norm.fit(Z_values)
print(f'Normal distribution with loc={loc:4.3f}, scale={scale:4.3f}')

Seasonal Kendall Test
The Seasonal Kendall test is an extension of the Mann-Kendall test designed to handle seasonality in the data. The Mann-Kendall test is non-parametric and does not assume a specific data distribution. The test assumes that the seasonal components (e.g., monthly or quarterly data) are independent and identically distributed.
Another version of the Mann-Kendall test considers periodicity in time series data. This test forms the significant trend detection window between 0.1 ppb/year and 0.2 ppb/year with the appropriate periodicity. However, a wrong periodicity value of 2 shows the same results as the general Mann-Kandal test.
import pymannkendall as mk
def mk_seasonal_test(data, period=12):
'''using pymannkendall library with a seasonal test method'''
result_seasonal = mk.seasonal_test(data.concentration, period=period)  # Example period

print(f"Hypothesis: {'Reject null hypothesis of no trend' if result_seasonal.h else 'Fail to reject null hypothesis of no trend'}")
print(f"p-value: {result_seasonal.p}\n")

2.
Implications of Unit Root
A time series with a unit root is non-stationary. Stationarity means that the statistical properties of the series (mean, variance, autocorrelation) are constant over time. Non-stationary time series with unit roots exhibit a time-dependent structure, meaning their behaviour changes over time, making prediction and analysis more complex.
A unit root process is often described as a random walk. Each value is a random step away from the previous value in a random walk. This makes the series unpredictable, and any shock to the series has a lasting effect.
3.
Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test
The Dickey-Fuller Test tests the null hypothesis that a unit root is present in an autoregressive model. The ADF Test extends the Dickey-Fuller test, including lagged differences to account for higher-order autocorrelation. The presence of a unit root in a time series indicates that the series has a stochastic trend, making it non-stationary.

4. Application of the ADF Test using the Python library
Using the Python library, I have plotted a histogram of the ADF test statistics of non-stationary datasets generated by a random walk process.
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller
# Function to generate a non-stationary time series (random walk)
def generate_random_walk(n):
return np.cumsum(np.random.normal(size=n))
# Number of simulations
num_simulations = 1000
sample_size = 100
adf_statistics = []
# Perform the ADF test on multiple random walk samples
for _ in range(num_simulations):
random_walk = generate_random_walk(sample_size)
result = adfuller(random_walk)
adf_statistics.append(result[0])
# Plot the distribution of the ADF statistics
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
plt.hist(adf_statistics, bins=50, density=True, alpha=0.6, color='g')
plt.xlabel('ADF Statistic')
plt.ylabel('Density')
plt.title('Distribution of ADF Statistic for Non-Stationary Time Series')
plt.axvline(x=np.percentile(adf_statistics, 2.5), color='r', linestyle='--', label='2.5th Percentile')
plt.axvline(x=np.percentile(adf_statistics, 97.5), color='b', linestyle='--', label='97.5th Percentile')
plt.legend();

Closing Remarks
Climate change is causing a wide range of extreme weather events worldwide. In 2021, North America experienced an unprecedented heat wave, with temperatures soaring above 40 degrees Celsius in many regions. This extreme heat led to wildfires, power outages and hundreds of deaths. This summer, China and South Korea are both experiencing severe flooding due to heavy rainfall linked to climate change.
I created toy datasets to simulate the time series of global methane concentrations and statistically detect trends. I explored statistical test methods such as ordinary least square regression, Mann-Kendall, seasonal Mann-Kendall and unit root tests. Using the toy datasets and test tools, I know that we should reduce the slope to 1/35 of the current slope if we want to get an insignificant trend in methane increase.
